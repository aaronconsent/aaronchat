# Hey Aaron! Voice Guide
### For aaron.chat, cold email, booked-jobs.com, social, and anything else that talks

**How to use this doc:** Paste the whole thing into any AI system as a `=== VOICE GUIDE ===` block, or hand it to a human writer. Sections 6 and 7 are the ones Aaron needs to red-pen hardest — that's where the voice actually lives.

---

## 1. Who's talking

Aaron Phillips. One guy in Coldspring, Texas, 20+ years deep in marketing, talking to another owner-operator who's got a truck, a crew, and a phone that isn't ringing enough. Not an agency "we." Not a brand. A person who answers his own phone and has strong opinions because he's earned them.

The reader should feel like they're getting the same straight talk Aaron would give a buddy at the poker table who asked "should I hire this marketing company?" — the answer you give when you've got no commission riding on it.

---

## 2. The profanity rules (the informal twist)

Profanity is a **highlighter, not a filler**. It marks the sentences that matter most. Used right, one "damn" does more work than three exclamation points. Used wrong, it's a guy trying too hard.

**The register:** PG-13. Approved: *damn, hell, ass / half-assed / kick your ass, crap, BS, bull, sucks, pissed off, screwed.* One notch stronger (*shit*) is allowed but rationed — treat it like the one f-bomb in a PG-13 movie: you get maybe one per page, and it better be load-bearing. Never anything stronger. Never slurs, never crude sexual language, never profanity aimed at a named person or company.

**Calibration note (from Aaron's real writing):** His natural baseline is ZERO profanity — five raw samples, not one curse word. So the highlighter rule isn't a restraint on a cusser; it's an added spice on a naturally clean voice. That makes each instance land even harder. When in doubt, leave it out — the samples prove the voice works without it.

**The placement law — emphasis, not conversation:**
- ✅ On the point of the sentence that carries the argument: *"You're not bad at marketing. You've been sold half-assed marketing."*
- ✅ On the villain, the stakes, or the hard truth: *"That lead you paid $80 for? They sold the same damn lead to four other roofers."*
- ❌ Never as seasoning in neutral sentences: not *"give me a damn call"* or *"check out our crap-ton of services."* If the sentence would survive fine without it, it doesn't get it.

**Frequency caps:** Max 2–3 instances per page. Zero is fine and common. If two show up in the same paragraph, cut one.

**Hard no-fly zones (never any profanity here):**
- Meta titles, meta descriptions, ad copy, anything Google/Meta crawls for ad approval — profanity gets ads disapproved and landing pages flagged
- The guarantee, pricing terms, contract/legal language — money talk stays clean so it reads as trustworthy, not salesy
- Schema/JSON-LD content
- Anything a client's customer might see (work we ship FOR clients follows THEIR voice, not this one)
- Email subject lines (deliverability + first impressions)

## 3. Lexicon — say this, not that

| Never say | Say instead |
|---|---|
| conversions, lead flow, pipeline | booked jobs, the phone ringing, work on the calendar |
| holistic, omnichannel, synergy, leverage (verb) | (nothing — these are banned, rewrite the sentence) |
| digital solutions, deliverables | the work, what I actually did, your website / your ads |
| utilize | use |
| best-in-class, cutting-edge, innovative | (show the result instead of claiming the adjective) |
| "reach out" | call me, text me |
| ROI-focused strategies | you make more than you spend, or I hear about it |
| "our team" | I / me (it's Aaron; if a contractor helps, name them) |
| SEO, PPC, LSA (unexplained) | showing up on Google, the ads, Google's pay-per-lead program — introduce the acronym only after the plain version |
| clients | contractors, or the trade by name (roofers, HVAC guys) |

**Numbers:** Casual amounts get casual treatment — *"fifteen hundred bucks," "eighty-dollar junk leads."* Exact commitments get exact digits — *"$1,500/mo," "answered in 12 seconds."* Round when talking, precise when promising.

**Contractions:** Always. "You're," "don't," "it's." A sentence without contractions reads like a lawyer wrote it.

## 4. Cadence & mechanics

- Short sentences. Then a shorter one. Then occasionally a long one that stacks up three or four things the reader already agrees with before landing the point.
- One-sentence paragraphs are legal and encouraged for the knockout line.
- Questions to open sections, statements to close them.
- **Ellipses are Aaron's real connector** — he trails "...." (four dots and all) between thoughts in emails and posts. Keep this in email/social where it's authentic; on the website, tighten most of them into dashes or periods, but let one or two survive per page so it still sounds like him.
- Dashes over semicolons. Semicolons are banned.
- **The equals-sign punchline** — "My life = X credits remaining." Legal in social posts and email, one per piece max. Not in website headers.
- **Numbered options his way:** "1.)" and "2.)" with the paren — that's his actual formatting when laying out choices in an email.
- Links dropped inline mid-sentence like they're just words. He does this constantly — don't quarantine URLs into buttons in email/social contexts.
- Sentence fragments allowed for punch. *"No contracts. No hostage websites. No BS."*
- 6th-grade reading level. If your uncle who roofs houses would squint at a word, swap it.
- Second person dominant ("you/your"), first person singular for Aaron ("I built," "I'll show you"). "We" only when it genuinely means Aaron + the reader.

## 5. Signature moves

1. **The oddly-specific stat** — precise numbers where round ones are expected. Credibility with a wink: a real, specific number ("answered in 12 seconds," "three towns, not the whole county") is more believable than 100% and funnier than "usually." Use one per page where a claim needs teeth. **(RETIRED: the "92% answer rate" example is killed — Aaron 2026-08. Keep the technique; only ever use a number Aaron will stand behind.)**
2. **Napkin math out loud** — walk the reader through the money right in the prose: *"50 leads at $2–4k per quote = $100,000 spent before they saw their first piece of work."* Aaron doesn't say "that's expensive," he shows the arithmetic and lets the reader gasp. This is his single best persuasion move — use it on junk-lead costs, agency contract math, and cost-per-booked-job.
3. **Admit what you can't do / haven't cracked** — *"I still have not cracked the code on B&R"... "I probably won't be able to turn out a fully interactive website, but I can get them headed in the right direction."* He volunteers his limits and unsolved problems, even when it kills his own deal (he once told a client there was no easy win after 20–30 hours of research). This is the deepest trust move in the voice — the website should do it too.
4. **The receipts move**: follow every claim with the verifiable thing. *"Don't take my word for it — here's the March task list from a real client."*
5. **Name the uncomfortable thing first**: open sections with the objection the reader is already thinking. *"You've been burned before. Probably twice."*
6. **The anti-pitch**: tell people when NOT to hire you, what the big agencies do better, and what things cost. Transparency IS the sales strategy. (Confirmed by his real emails — he closes deals by talking people out of bad ones.)
7. **Poker is a place, not a metaphor.** Correction from the draft: Aaron networks AT the poker table (*"Nice meeting you last night at poker"*) but doesn't talk in card metaphors. Reference the table as his community and where deals start — drop the "win the hand" wordplay almost entirely.
8. **Personal-context openers** — he starts from the human connection, not the pitch: *"Nice meeting you last night at poker."* *"Blast from the past: found a nearly 20-year-old server quote in my mailbox."* Cold pages can't always do this, but About, email, and social should.
9. **"Howdy" is real.** He literally opens emails with *"Howdy Michael."* Un-banned. The line stands against cartoon-country ("y'all reckon") — but Howdy, warm closers (*"Have a fantastic Friday," "Later," "Warm Regards"*), and rotating casual sign-offs are authentically his.
10. **Dry parenthetical asides** — the joke lives in the parens: *"(who probably doesn't believe in LinkedIn)."* One or two per piece.
11. **Job-site respect**: talk about their work like it's skilled work, because it is. Never punch down at the trades. The villain is always the marketing industry, never the contractor.
12. **Music timing** (subtle): Aaron's a piano player — vary the sentence lengths like a groove, don't march in 4/4.

## 10. Numbers, benchmarks & proof — sources of truth (added 2026-08, from the CPL-calculator methodology)

The lead-cost calculator (`/stats/` explains it in full) set the standard for how we use *any* market number, anywhere — site, email, social, sales calls. **The rule: a number is either sourced or it's an illustration, and we say which.** Never a third option.

**Our canonical benchmark sources (cite these by name + year, link to the primary article):**
- **Google Ads cost-per-lead** — LocaliQ *Home Services Search Advertising Benchmarks 2025* (3,211 US campaigns, Apr 2024–Mar 2025). This is the primary — pull from the article itself, never a re-citation (re-citations misreport the numbers).
- **Google LSA cost-per-lead** — SearchLight Digital *LSA CPL by Trade 2026* (888 contractors, 126,650 leads). Firm for HVAC/plumbing/electrical/roofing; blended $53 elsewhere.
- **Facebook/Meta cost-per-lead** — LocaliQ *Facebook Ad Benchmarks 2025* (Home & Home Improvement, $41 CPL).
- **Local variation** — DataForSEO Google Ads Search Volume (Keyword Planner CPC), used **only as a state-vs-national ratio**, never as an absolute price (Keyword-Planner CPC runs 3–5× real). ZIP resolves to **state**, not literal ZIP — say so.
- **Organic** — **$7/lead** = our real cost to resolve an anonymous visitor via ConsentResolve. A cost we pay, not a benchmark.

**Data-integrity rules (these are hard, same family as "No fake reviews. No made-up numbers."):**
1. **Every figure carries a named, dated source — or an explicit "N/A."** If a channel doesn't cover a trade (e.g. Google LSA doesn't run solar), the card says *"N/A — not available for this trade,"* never a guessed number.
2. **Tier every number** so the reader knows how solid it is: **firm** (audited dataset, n>500), **directional** (agency-published), **proxy** (borrowed from an adjacent trade — and we name which). Proxies are defined up front, never invented mid-claim.
3. **Two independent sources per channel** where possible; re-citations of one study count as one.
4. **Sanity bounds** — Google Ads CPL $40–$350, LSA $15–$200, Facebook $10–$120. A number outside gets a second source before it ships.
5. **Match the vintage** — don't blend a 2023 figure with a 2026 one without saying so.
6. **Show the math out loud** (this is already a signature move) — but label illustrations as illustrations ("a sample month, not a guarantee").

**Conflict flagged & resolved:** Section 5's signature move *"the oddly-specific stat (92%)"* now has a guardrail. Oddly-specific numbers are great voice **only when they're real and sourced.** The 92% stat itself was invented-for-flavor and Aaron killed it (§9). Going forward: keep the oddly-specific *texture* ("$6.72M of spend across 126,650 leads"), but pull it from a real source — never manufacture precision to sound credible. Specificity earns trust only if it survives a click to the source.
